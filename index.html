“If everything ends, what’s the point?”

You do not have to force yourself to believe that life has a grand, permanent meaning. This page helps you notice the rule your mind may be using, understand the part of you that carries it, and choose how you want to live while uncertainty is still here.

“If it doesn’t last forever, it doesn’t matter.”

Start here

The problem may not be the thought itself.

A mind can turn an observation—“everything is temporary”—into a rule: “therefore nothing matters.” ACT does not require you to prove that rule wrong. It asks whether living under it gives you more freedom, connection, and choice—or less.

1

Notice the rule

“My mind says temporary = meaningless.” Naming the rule creates a little space around it.

2

Notice the protection

A detached or hopeless part may be trying to protect you from grief, disappointment, vulnerability, or the pain of caring.

3

Notice the cost

Ask what the rule saves you from today—and what it may take away from you over months or years.

Parts-informed

Meet the part that says, “Nothing matters.”

You do not need to get rid of this part. Try approaching it with curiosity. A protective part can have a good intention even when its strategy leaves you feeling numb, disconnected, or stuck.

ACT lens

Move from “Is it true?” to “Is it workable?”

Some questions cannot be settled with certainty. ACT shifts attention from proving a worldview to observing what that worldview does in your life.

When the rule is in charge

  • Withdraw from people or activities
  • Stop investing because loss feels inevitable
  • Wait for certainty before choosing
  • Use numbness as protection
→

When you have more choice

  • Let the thought exist without obeying it
  • Choose how to participate today
  • Care without requiring permanence
  • Allow uncertainty and still take one step

A 5% question

Instead of asking the hopeless part to disappear, ask whether it would be willing to step back 5% while you try one small action.

What to notice afterward

Did the thought vanish? It does not have to. Notice whether your behavior became a little less controlled by it.

Keep in mind

This is not positive thinking.

No forced optimism

You can acknowledge death, uncertainty, and impermanence without pretending they are easy.

No arguing with parts

A hopeless part may soften more through understanding than through pressure to “think differently.”

No requirement to feel better first

You can make a small values-based choice while sadness, fear, or meaninglessness is still present.
